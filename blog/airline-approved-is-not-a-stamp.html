'Airline approved' is not a stamp — here's what to check

I once watched a family get stopped at check-in because their carrier was two centimetres too tall. The bag had 'airline approved' printed on the tag in large letters. The airline disagreed. There was a quiet, awkward rearrangement of luggage in the middle of the terminal while their dog watched from inside the carrier, wondering what was happening. They eventually got through, but the first hour of their holiday was spent apologising to other passengers. I have never forgotten it.

There is no universal approval

That phrase on a product page is marketing shorthand, not an official certification. There is no global authority that stamps carriers as approved. Every airline sets its own under-seat dimensions, and those dimensions vary by aircraft — the same airline can have different limits on a short-haul regional jet and a long-haul widebody. The number printed on a bag is usually a best guess at what most airlines accept most of the time.

Your job is to check the airline you are actually flying with, on the day you book, and again a week before departure. Find the pet policy page, note the maximum height, width and depth for in-cabin carriers, and write them down. Policies change with aircraft rotations, and a friendly phone call to customer service is worth more than a hundred reviews. If the airline will not give you a clear answer, assume the smaller dimension and plan around it.

The phrase 'airline approved' is still useful as a rough signal — it usually means the bag is in the right general size range and has enough ventilation. Just treat it as a starting point, not a promise. The two centimetres in that story were the whole problem.

Measure the bag, not the box

The most common mistake is measuring the packaging or trusting the product description instead of the object. Take a tape measure to the actual carrier. Measure the outside, at the widest points, including wheels, handles, feet, and any protruding clips. Soft carriers can be squashed a little to fit under a seat; hard-sided ones cannot. If you fly often, that distinction decides which bag you should own.

Then add a margin. A carrier that exactly matches the published limit is a carrier that will fail if the measurement was taken slightly differently at the gate. Two centimetres of slack has saved more trips than any feature I can think of. If your pet's comfort allows it, choose a bag comfortably under the limit rather than one that pushes right up to it.

You also need to check the weight rule. Some airlines include the pet and carrier in your hand luggage allowance; others do not. A hard shell is heavier before anything goes inside, and on a strict airline that extra kilo matters. Weigh the packed carrier at home, with your pet inside if they will tolerate it for a minute, and know the number before you leave for the airport.

Hard shell or soft sided

This is the decision people agonise over, and it really comes down to how you travel. A soft-sided carrier is lighter, flexes to fit awkward spaces, and is easier to stow under a seat. It is also easier for a determined animal to damage, and it offers less protection if a bag gets dropped or stacked on. For a calm, small pet on occasional flights, soft sides are usually the practical choice.

A hard shell gives better protection and holds its shape, which matters on long journeys and when your carrier is handled by other people. It is easier to clean after an accident, and the structure makes a nervous dog feel less exposed. The trade-offs are weight and zero flexibility — if it is too tall, no amount of pushing will make it fit. There is a middle ground in carriers with a rigid base and a flexible top, but they are not ideal for every airline. Whichever you choose, remember that airlines sometimes require the animal to be visible or reachable. A fully enclosed hard case with tiny windows can cause problems on check-in even when the dimensions are perfect, so check the airline's rules about ventilation openings before you commit.

The details that survive a real trip

Look at the latches, the handle attachment, and the door. These are the parts that break, and they are the parts nobody photographs. A door that can pop open under pressure is a genuine emergency at thirty thousand feet. Two secure latches, a door that locks positively, and a handle anchored into the shell rather than glued onto it are worth more than a dozen pockets.

Ventilation needs the same scrutiny. Air circulates differently under a seat than it does in a cabin, and a carrier with holes on only one side can leave your pet in a pocket of stale air. Check that there are openings on at least two opposing sides, and that the pet can see out. A dog who can watch the cabin is generally calmer than one who cannot. Slide the carrier partway under the seat before you board and see whether the openings remain uncovered once it is in position — this is a common oversight.

The interior is worth thinking about too. Put down an absorbent pad rather than a loose towel that can bunch up, and bring a familiar-smelling item from home. Freeze a small water bottle the night before so it thaws slowly during the flight, and attach it securely so it does not roll around. None of this affects whether the carrier is approved, but all of it affects whether your pet arrives relaxed.

The day of the flight

Get to the airport early. Not the usual early — genuinely early. Check-in staff deal with pet travel inconsistently, and the extra time lets you sort out a problem without watching the gate close. Have the carrier, the booking reference, any health documents, and the airline's pet policy printed or saved on your phone. Do not rely on the agent knowing the rules by heart.

At security, you will usually need to take your pet out of the carrier. Practise this at home so it is not a wrestling match in front of a queue. A harness and a short lead make it much easier to keep hold of an animal who would rather bolt. Put the lead on before you open the carrier door, every time, without exception. The number of pets lost at airports because someone opened a door first is far higher than people realise.

During the flight, keep the carrier under the seat in front rather than at your feet if the rules allow. It is more stable there and less likely to be kicked. Speak to your pet quietly at intervals, and do not open the door in the cabin — a scared animal in a pressurized aircraft is a nightmare to recapture. If you need to check on them, use a phone camera angled into the carrier rather than unzipping it.

Do a rehearsal

Before the real flight, take your carrier to a cinema, a train, or a friend's house. See if it fits under a seat. See how your pet behaves in it for two hours. See how you manage carrying it while also holding a passport, a boarding pass, and a coffee. The cheapest mistakes to fix are the ones you make at home, and the rehearsal turns a list of worries into a short list of things you still need to sort out.

If flying is not the right choice

It is worth saying that flying with a pet is not always the kindest option, whatever carrier you buy. For a short journey, a car or train can be calmer, more predictable, and far less stressful for an animal who dislikes noise and unfamiliar handling. If you are moving house with a dog and the drive is a day, the drive may genuinely be the better choice. A carrier still matters on that journey — a secured crate or a well-fitted seat belt harness keeps everyone safe — but the pressure to find the perfect airline carrier disappears.

When flying is unavoidable, book direct flights wherever possible. Each connection is another security line, another gate change, and another chance for a well-meaning member of staff to misapply the rules. Tell the airline at booking that you are travelling with a pet in the cabin, and again at check-in. Bring printed copies of the policy. Being politely persistent with documentation has got more pets onto flights than any argument about the word 'approved' printed on a bag.

An airline approved pet carrier is really just a carrier you have checked carefully. Do the measuring yourself, check the specific airline, read the policy, and leave a margin. Then the phrase on the tag stops mattering, because you already know it will fit.

What size carrier fits under an airplane seat?

It varies by airline and aircraft, but a common limit is around 40 x 30 x 20 cm. Always check your airline's published pet policy before booking, and measure the outside of the carrier including wheels, handles and clips.

Can my dog fly in a hard-sided carrier?

Yes, if it fits the airline's size rules and your dog can stand, turn around and lie down comfortably. Confirm the airline allows rigid carriers in the cabin — a few only accept soft-sided bags.

What should I put in a carrier for a flight?

An absorbent pad, a familiar-smelling blanket or t-shirt, and a water source that will not spill — a frozen bottle works well. Avoid loose toys that can roll around, and attach a lead to your pet before opening the carrier at security.
