Why cats knead (and why it means so much)

Somewhere tonight, a cat is pressing its paws into a blanket, alternating left right left right, eyes half closed, completely absorbed. Biscuit making, kneading, making bread. Whatever your family calls it, it is one of the strangest and sweetest things cats do.

Where it comes from

Kneading starts in the first days of life. Kittens push against their mother to bring milk down, and the motion gets wired to a feeling of total safety and fullness. Most cats carry the habit into adulthood, the way some people still fidget with a necklace when they are relaxed.

The usual theory holds that it simply feels good to keep doing. Comfort behavior. A ritual from a safe time, replayed in safe moments.

Why they knead you

Because you are the safe moment. A cat kneading your lap is telling you where it has filed you, next to milk and warmth and the earliest good feelings it ever had. There is no higher review in the cat world.

Soft surfaces invite it too. That is why blankets, beds, and laundry piles get the treatment. Cats also have scent glands in their paw pads, so kneading leaves a quiet personal mark on a place or a person.

The claws, gently

Yes, it hurts when the program includes claw deployment. Never punish it; the behavior is pure affection. Trim the nails on schedule and put a thick blanket over your lap when the biscuits start, and everybody wins.

You can tell a lot about a product by whether it gets kneaded. In our sample room, the fabrics that survive a week of biscuit testing are the ones that go into production. Loose weaves pull apart under kneading claws within days; a tight, dense knit takes it for years. Cats are brutally honest QA engineers.

So when your cat sets to work on you, slow and rhythmic and shameless, know what it is saying. It is saying you remind it of the very first safety it ever knew. Not bad for a Tuesday evening.
