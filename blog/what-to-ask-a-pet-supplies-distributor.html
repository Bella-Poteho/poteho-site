What to ask a pet supplies distributor before your first order

The first wholesale order is the one that teaches you the most, usually by costing you something. I made the expensive version of this mistake — ordering a mixed container straight from a catalogue without asking the questions that mattered. The products were fine. The assumptions were not. I had assumed minimums were flexible, that samples would be credited, and that the person who answered the first email would still be answering six months later. Two of those three were wrong.

Start with the product, not the price list

A good distributor can tell you what a product is made of, what sizes it comes in, who it is for, and what the packaging looks like. If every answer is 'best quality' with no detail behind it, keep asking. Ask for the fabric, the hardware, the net weight, the folded size, the colours in stock. These specifics tell you whether the person on the other end knows the range or is simply forwarding a spreadsheet they did not write.

Ask which products are actually in stock versus made to order. That distinction affects your lead time more than anything else, and it is often glossed over in the first conversation. A supplier who says everything is available needs to back that up with a realistic production windows. If a company claims three-day delivery on custom colours, be sceptical until you see it in writing.

Then look at consistency. If the catalogue shows twelve models but the website only details six, ask about the other six. Gaps in a range usually mean something — discontinued lines, unreliable supply, or products the supplier does not actually hold. None of these are dealbreakers, but you want to know before you build a range around them.

Ask about minimums in plain numbers

Minimum order quantities are where new buyers get trapped. Ask the MOQ per item and the MOQ per order, in actual units. Then ask whether you can mix models within one shipment. A supplier who can put twelve carriers, twenty leashes and ten coats into one order is very different from one who wants five hundred of a single SKU. If you are testing a market, flexibility at the start is worth more than a slightly better unit price.

Ask what happens when a size or colour sells out. Can you reorder that one item quickly, or do you have to place another full minimum? How long does a reorder take? These questions feel tedious on a first call and become urgent when your best-selling size runs out in the middle of a season.

Packaging minimums are worth checking too. Some suppliers will only produce custom packaging at high volumes, which means your first order ships in neutral bags. That is fine if you know it in advance and terrible if you were planning a branded launch. Ask early and build it into your budget.

Samples are a conversation, not a favour

Always ask for samples before a bulk order, and ask plainly who pays. Many suppliers send samples free and charge only shipping; some credit the sample cost against your first order; a few charge full price and refund it later. All of these are normal. What is not normal is a refusal to let you touch the product before ordering hundreds of units.

When the samples arrive, check them properly. Look at the stitching at stress points, run the zips, test the clips, and smell the fabric. Check that the colours match the catalogue, because they often do not — screens lie and dye lots vary. Measure the sample against the stated dimensions. If the measurements are wrong on a sample, imagine what they will be like in a production run.

Give the sample to someone else to look at too. A fresh pair of eyes catches things you stop noticing after handling the same bag for ten minutes. And keep the samples. They are your reference when the bulk order arrives, and the only reliable way to answer the question 'is this what we agreed on?'

Ask who you actually deal with

Will you have one contact or a different person each time? What hours do they work, and in which time zone? What happens when a shipment is delayed — who tells you, and how quickly? How are defective units handled? These questions feel awkward on a first call and become very important on the third order, when something has inevitably gone slightly wrong.

A supplier with a single point of contact is easier to work with, but check whether that person can actually make decisions or has to refer everything upward. Ask who signs off on a credit or a replacement. If nobody can give you a straight answer, you will discover the answer during a dispute, which is the worst possible time.

Communication style tells you a lot. A distributor who replies within a day, answers in full sentences, and admits when they do not know something is usually reliable. One who sends one-word replies, disappears for a week, or promises everything without checking is showing you how future problems will be handled.

Shipping, duties and the fine print

Ask who arranges shipping and who pays for it, and get the incoterm in writing. FOB, EXW and CIF mean very different things in practice, and the price per unit is meaningless until you know what is included. Ask for a full landed-cost estimate to your door, including freight, insurance, duties and local delivery. A cheaper unit price can disappear fast once freight is added.

Find out how the goods are packed. Carton size and weight per carton affect your shipping cost directly, and products that fold flat can save a remarkable amount of space. Ask how many units fit in a carton and what the carton dimensions are. If your supplier cannot answer, ask them to send a photo of a packed carton.

Then ask about documents. You will need a commercial invoice, a packing list, and possibly certificates depending on your market and the materials involved. A distributor who has shipped to your country before will know exactly what is required. One who has not may learn at your expense, so ask for a reference from a customer in your region.

Quality control and what happens when it goes wrong

Ask whether the supplier inspects goods before shipping, and whether you can request photos or a report before payment. Some will send a pre-shipment inspection with measurements and images. That is a good sign. If they say defects never happen, that is a less good sign — experienced suppliers know they do and have a process for catching them.

Get the complaint process in writing. What is the window for reporting a defect after delivery? Who pays for replacements? Do you return the faulty item or is it credited against the next order? What counts as a defect versus normal variation in a handmade product? A clear answer here protects both sides, and a vague one will be interpreted in the supplier's favour when it matters.

Our full range covers carriers, leashes, apparel, beds, fences and rehabilitation products, and we would rather explain the details now than fix a misunderstanding after a container has shipped. A distributor who welcomes these questions is generally one you can work with for years.

Payment terms and building a second order

Ask about payment terms early, because they shape your cash flow more than the unit price does. Many suppliers ask for a deposit before production and the balance before shipping. Some offer credit after a few successful orders. Find out what the terms are for the first order, what unlocks better terms, and whether there is any discount for earlier payment. A supplier who is flexible here is often easier to grow with than one who insists on full payment upfront forever.

Think about the second order while you are placing the first. A distributor who can tell you which products reorder most often, which have the longest lead time, and which are being discontinued is thinking about your business rather than just this shipment. Ask what the typical reorder cycle looks like for a buyer in your market. If they have no answer, you may be dealing with someone who moves goods rather than builds ranges — fine for a one-off, less useful if you plan to still be here in three years.

Finally, start small enough that a mistake is survivable. The temptation on a first order is to fill a container because the unit price looks better. Resist it. A smaller mixed order teaches you which products actually sell, how the packaging holds up in transit, and whether the supplier does what they said they would. You can always scale up once you have answers. Most of the expensive lessons in wholesale come from ordering with confidence before you have evidence, and almost none come from ordering too little.

A pet supplies wholesale distributor should make your job easier, not just move boxes. Judge them on how clearly they answer the boring questions — the ones about minimums, samples, packaging and what happens when something goes wrong.

What is a reasonable minimum order for a new buyer?

It varies widely by supplier and product. Many will accept a mixed first order of a few dozen units across several models. Be cautious of very high minimums on a first order — ask whether you can start small and scale up.

Should I always request samples before ordering wholesale?

Yes. Check fabric, stitching, hardware, zips, measurements and packaging yourself. A sample costs far less than a container of something you cannot sell, and it becomes your reference when the bulk order arrives.

What is the difference between FOB and EXW pricing?

EXW means you collect the goods at the factory and arrange everything from there. FOB means the supplier delivers the goods onto a ship at a named port, with you handling freight from that point. Ask for a landed cost to your door so you can compare suppliers fairly.
