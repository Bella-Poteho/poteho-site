Why I stopped holding the leash on morning runs

For years I ran with the leash looped over one hand and my phone in the other. It worked right up until it didn't — a sudden squirrel, a tightened grip, and a jolt that went straight through my wrist. I came home more sore from the leash than from the run. Worse, I had spent the whole route bracing for the next pull instead of paying attention to where we were going. That is a strange way to enjoy a morning.

What 'hands free' actually changes

A hands free dog leash moves the connection point from your hand to your waist. That sounds like a small change and it is not. The difference is balance. When the pull comes from a belt around your hips, your arms stay loose, your shoulders stay down, and your stride stays normal. You stop acting as an anchor and start moving as a pair. For anyone who runs with a dog who is stronger than them, or just more enthusiastic, this changes the whole experience.

There is a learning curve, and I should be honest about it. The first week I kept reaching down for a handle that wasn't there. I also over-corrected at corners, because I was not used to steering with my hips rather than my hand. By the second week it clicked. I had my phone in one hand and water in the other, my dog settled into a rhythm beside me, and the run felt like a run again rather than a negotiation.

The other thing nobody tells you: a waist leash gives your dog more consistent feedback. When you hold a lead, your grip tightens and loosens without you noticing. The signal your dog receives is unpredictable. A belt-mounted connection stays at the same height and same tension, which makes it easier for a dog to learn where 'too far' is. Consistency is the whole basis of good lead manners, and this setup gives you more of it for free.

The reflective stripe matters more than you think

Most people buy a running leash for the walk, then use it at six in the morning in January when it is still properly dark. A reflective stripe stitched along the length does nothing for your dog directly. What it does is make the two of you visible as one object, at roughly hip height, which is exactly where a driver's headlights are pointing. A reflective collar on a low dog can disappear behind a kerb. A stripe along a leash that runs up to your waist cannot.

Check how the reflective material is applied, though. Printed-on silver looks bright in a product photo but rubs off after a few months of cable-style movement against your leg. Stitched reflective thread lasts far longer. Our Pet Running Leash has the stripe integrated into the webbing instead of printed on top, for exactly this reason. It costs a little more to make and it is the one upgrade I would never skip for a city dog.

Where a waist leash does not belong

I still use a short standard lead in crowds, at busy crossings, and around other dogs. A hands free setup gives you less precise control in tight spaces, and pretending otherwise is how someone gets tripped. If your dog lunges at passing cyclists, a belt-mounted leash means the reaction happens faster than you can unclip. You cannot step on a leash that is attached to your waist, which is a technique most experienced walkers use without thinking.

The same goes for narrow pavements, slippery surfaces, and anywhere with a real drop. Keep a short lead in your pocket for those moments. A hands free leash is a tool for open routes — parks, canals, quiet streets, trails — not a universal replacement. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

There is also a simple physical limit. A waist leash works because your body weight is the counterweight. If your dog is large enough to move you rather than the other way round, no leash design will fix that. In that case, a front-clip harness and proper training come first, and the hands free leash comes later as a reward for good behaviour rather than a solution to bad behaviour.

Choosing the right belt and length

Not all waist leashes are the same. The belt itself should be wide enough that it does not dig in, and it should stay put when you sprint. A narrow strap that rolls over itself is miserable after a kilometre. Look for a belt that can be adjusted while you are wearing it, because you will want to change the fit between a jacket in winter and a t-shirt in summer.

Length matters too. Too short and your dog is forced into your shadow, which is unpleasant for both of you. Too long and you get a bungee effect where your dog runs ahead, hits the end, and pulls you forward. The useful range for running is roughly one and a half to two metres of slack, enough for your dog to find a natural stride without disappearing around a corner. A bungee section can help absorb sudden pulls, but it also hides the tension signal your dog relies on, so I prefer a flat webbing lead with a bit of give in the material rather than an elastic insert.

The clip is worth checking as well. It is the single point of failure in the whole system. A cheap snap that sticks or has a weak gate will eventually open at the wrong moment. Run your thumb over it, open and close it a few times, and look for a proper swivel so the leash does not twist into a rope as your dog changes direction.

Training your dog to walk on a waist leash

Do not start with a run. Start in your living room. Put the belt on, clip the leash, and stand still for a minute while you give your dog a treat for checking in with you. Then walk a few steps indoors. The novelty of the setup is interesting to a dog, and you want that interest associated with calm behaviour rather than with the excitement of leaving the house.

Move outside to a quiet street for the first few sessions. Keep the sessions short — five minutes is plenty. Reward your dog whenever the leash stays loose. If it goes tight, stop and wait rather than pulling. This is standard loose-lead training, but you are now doing it with your hands free, which means you have to notice the tension through your body instead of through your palm. That takes a few days to get used to.

Once your dog walks calmly on a short route, add distance gradually. Add speed last. A dog who is fine walking beside you on a waist leash can still find a run exciting enough to forget their manners, so build up from a slow jog rather than jumping straight into your usual pace. And keep treats in a pocket you can actually reach. This sounds obvious until you are standing on a street corner with a treat bag clipped to the waist belt you cannot open one-handed.

Care, maintenance and when to replace it

A leash lives a hard life. It gets wet, muddy, dragged through gravel, and left in a hot car. Rinse the webbing after a salty or muddy run and let it dry before you coil it up. Check the stitching around the belt loops and the clip attachment every few weeks. Those are the two places where load concentrates, and fraying there is a clear signal to retire the leash.

Replace it sooner rather than later if you see damage. A leash is cheap compared to the alternative. Reflective material that has worn dull is another good reason — the safety feature has quietly stopped working even though the leash still looks fine in daylight. I keep an older one in the car as a spare and replace the main one each spring.

What changed for my dog, not just for me

I spent the first week thinking about my own comfort and barely noticed what the leash was doing for my dog. Then I watched a clip of us running from a friend's phone. My dog was trotting at a steady distance, checking back every few seconds, and I was not constantly tightening and releasing my grip. To a dog, that difference is huge. A hand-held lead transmits every tiny movement of your wrist — the phone shift, the coffee sip, the scratch of an itch — and a sensitive dog reads each one as a possible signal. A waist connection filters all that noise out. What is left is a clean, consistent line of communication.

My dog also stopped trying to lead. When the person holding the lead is visibly bracing, some dogs take that as permission to pull, because the human has already conceded the tension. With the attachment at my waist and my arms free, the whole dynamic changed without me running a single formal training session. That is not magic — it is just fewer accidental signals. But it is a good reminder that a lot of lead problems are really handling problems in disguise.

If your morning run currently involves clenching a leash for three kilometres, try moving the attachment to your waist and giving yourself a week to adjust. You probably won't go back. Just remember the short lead in your pocket for the crossings — that one habit will save you more trouble than any feature on the leash itself.

Are hands free leashes safe for strong dogs?

They are safe for dogs who already walk reasonably on a lead. A strong puller can still take you off balance, so a waist leash is not a substitute for training. Pair it with a front-clip harness, work on loose-lead walking, and keep a short lead for crowded or narrow places.

Can I use a hands free leash for hiking?

Yes, on open trails where you want your hands free for poles or a map. Keep a short lead accessible for narrow paths, river crossings and steep descents, where close control matters more than convenience.

How long should a running leash be?

Roughly one and a half to two metres of slack gives most dogs enough room to settle into a natural stride without running ahead. Shorter leads are better for training and busy streets; much longer ones create a delayed tug that is unpleasant for both of you.
