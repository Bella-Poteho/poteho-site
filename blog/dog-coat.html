A dog coat is really about the walk after the rain stops

The rain is not the problem. The problem is the twenty minutes after, when your dog is wet, the wind picks up, and you are walking home with an animal who has suddenly decided they are done. I have done that walk more times than I can count — the slowed pace, the lifted paw, the look back toward the car. A good coat is less about staying dry in the downpour and more about staying comfortable on the way back.

In this guide I will walk through what actually matters when you are looking at a dog coat, where the usual mistakes happen, and how to tell a piece that will last from one that will end up in a drawer.

Measuring a dog correctly

The most common mistake is measuring over a thick winter coat rather than the body underneath, or measuring while the dog is squirming and the tape has gone crooked. Do it on a calm dog, ideally after a walk when they are settled. Run the tape from the base of the neck — where a collar sits — to the base of the tail, not the tip. Then measure the chest at its widest point, usually just behind the front legs. Between sizes, a broad deep-chested dog usually needs the larger size, a lean long-legged dog the smaller. You should be able to slip two fingers between the fabric and your dog's body.

Looking after your pet through the seasons

Needs change with the weather and with age. A piece that is perfect in summer may be wrong in winter, and a young animal's requirements are not an older pet's. It is worth having a small amount of equipment that adapts — layers rather than one heavy item, adjustable straps rather than fixed sizes, designs that work indoors and out. Thinking ahead to the next season prevents the annual rush to replace everything, and it usually costs less. A little planning in a quiet week saves a lot of decision-making in a busy one.

Cleaning is part of ownership

Anything that touches a pet gets dirty. That is not a flaw, it is the job. The useful question is how easily the piece can be cleaned and how quickly it dries. Removable, washable parts are worth paying for. Surfaces that wipe down matter. Anything that has to be soaked, scrubbed or left for a day will be cleaned less often than it should be. Build a simple routine — a quick wipe after use, a proper clean once a week — and the piece will last longer and smell better. Ignore it and no amount of quality will save it.

Reflective detail is not decoration

Winter walks happen in the dark. If your dog is low to the ground and dark coloured, they are almost invisible from a distance. A reflective strip will not make your dog safe on its own — nothing replaces a short lead near roads — but it gives drivers a chance to see you both earlier. I treat it as non-negotiable between October and March. Check where the strip sits: a band around the belly is hidden from most angles, while a strip along the back or down both sides is visible from behind and from the side, which covers the situations you actually encounter on a walk. Stitched reflective thread beats printed silver for longevity.

When an upgrade makes sense

You do not need to replace equipment because a newer version exists. You need to replace it when it no longer does the job: when a strap stretches past adjustment, when cleaning stops working, when your pet has grown, or when a small failure becomes a repeated one. Keep using a piece until that point and you get real value from it. When you do upgrade, take the lessons from the old one with you — what worked, what annoyed you, and which feature you never used. That makes the next choice much sharper.

Ask someone who uses it, not just someone who sells it

Reviews and recommendations are useful when they come from people in a similar situation. Look for the details: the size of their dog, how often they use the piece, what failed and when. That is far more informative than a star rating. If you can, talk to other owners at a park or a show. Most people are happy to explain what they bought and why. A supplier who can point to real use cases rather than just a catalogue is telling you something useful about the product as well.

Reading your dog's first reaction

Watch what your dog does in the first minute. A dog who freezes, hunches, or tries to back out is telling you the coat is uncomfortable even if the measurements look right. Take it off, check for anything pulling at the armpits or throat, and try the next size. Do not power through it. A coat your dog tolerates is still useful; a coat they panic in is not. Some dogs need a few short sessions at home with treats before they accept a garment at all. That patience pays off over a whole winter of walks.

The value of consistency

Once you find equipment that works, there is a lot to be said for staying with it. Replacing everything each season costs more and introduces new variables into a routine your pet has learned. Consistency helps animals — familiar shapes, familiar smells, familiar routines. It also helps you, because you stop thinking about the equipment and just use it. That is the quiet argument for buying carefully once rather than casually many times, and it is the principle we try to build into everything at Poteho.

Reading a size chart properly

A size chart is only useful if you measure the way the chart expects. Read the notes, not just the numbers. Some charts want the animal standing, some want a relaxed sitting position; some measure the body, others the garment. Use a soft tape, keep it level, and take the measurement twice. If you are between sizes, the answer depends on the build of your pet and on what the piece does — there is no universal rule to size up or down. When in doubt, send the measurements to the supplier and ask. A good one will answer.

Washing and reproofing

A winter coat gets filthy with mud, road salt and general grime. Wipe it down after a walk for day-to-day mess. A full wash should be rare — once or twice a season — because frequent washing wears out water-repellent treatments faster than actual use does. Use a gentle cycle and mild detergent, never fabric softener, which leaves a film that interferes with water resistance. Hang it to dry completely before storing; a coat folded away damp will smell by autumn. If the water resistance fades, a reproofing spray can bring the shell back — test it on a small area first.

Safety is not the same as complexity

More straps, buckles and adjustments do not automatically make a piece safer. Often they create more ways to fit it wrongly. The safest equipment is usually the simplest piece that fits correctly and stays where it is meant to. Check that nothing can catch on a paw or a tooth, that edges are finished, and that any hardware is strong enough for the animal. Then check the fit again after a week of real use. Simple, well-fitted and well-maintained beats complicated and badly adjusted every time.

Making the final decision

By the time you have measured, checked the construction, read a few honest reviews and considered your own routine, the decision is usually obvious. If it is not, it often means two options are genuinely close and either will work — in which case choose the one that is easier to clean or store. There is rarely a single perfect answer in this category. There is a right answer for your pet and your life, and you are now in a good position to recognise it. When a dog does not need a coat

Not every dog needs clothing. Breed, coat thickness, size, age and temperature all matter. A husky in a mild winter is uncomfortable in a jacket; a shivering whippet is not. Watch your dog's behaviour rather than the thermometer: slowing down, lifting paws, shivering or reluctance to continue are signs it is time to go in or put a layer on. Overdressing is a real mistake too — a thick-coated dog in a warm coat can overheat quickly. How to read the small print

The details people skip are usually the ones that matter. Read the measurements twice, check what is included in the price, and look for the conditions attached to any claim. A size chart without a measurement method is a guess. A material named without a percentage is half a description. When you find a piece whose small print is clear and specific, that itself is useful information — it suggests the maker expects the product to be examined rather than glanced at. Take the same approach with the dog coat you are considering and the choice becomes much easier.

What experience teaches that a catalogue cannot

There is a gap between how a product reads on a page and how it behaves after a fortnight of real use. Catalogues cannot tell you that a zip catches if you pull at an angle, or that a strap slides when it is damp, or that a fabric holds on to smells. Those things only show up in use. That is why I put more weight on detailed long-term reviews and on the opinions of owners I trust than on the specification list. When you evaluate a dog coat, look for evidence of how it ages, not just how it arrives.

What should I look for in a dog coat?

Focus on fit, construction and how the piece behaves in real use rather than on the product photograph. Measure carefully, check the parts that take the load, and read the size chart against your own animal rather than a breed guess. A slightly higher-quality piece that reorders well usually costs less over a season than a cheap replacement.

How do I know if dog coat is right for my pet?

Start with your pet's size, temperament and the way you will actually use it. Watch how they react in the first few minutes — reluctance, freezing or trying to remove something usually means a fit or introduction problem, not a difficult animal. Give any new piece a short, calm trial before a full day of use.

How do I care for it so it lasts?

Clean it gently and let it dry fully before storing. Check the stress points — straps, clips, zips and stitching — every few weeks, since those fail long before the main material does. Store it somewhere dry and out of direct heat, and replace it early if you see fraying or damage that affects safety.
