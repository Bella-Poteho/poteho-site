Introducing a new pet to the family (slowly)

The movies have lied to us about this for a hundred years. In the film, the new puppy and the old dog sniff once, chase each other around a tree, and are friends for life by the fade-out. In real houses, the fade-out takes three to eight weeks, and the speed at which you go is the single biggest factor in whether it works.

The principle

Your resident pet did not agree to this. From its point of view, a stranger has arrived claiming resources it spent years securing. The goal of every good introduction is to make the stranger boring before it makes it close. Fear fades with repetition; that is the entire trick, and there is no shortcut around it.

Dog to dog

First meeting on neutral ground, a park neither dog considers home, both on loose leashes, walks parallel with distance. Let them arc past each other a few times over days. Home meetings come after, in the yard if there is one, short and calm, ending before anyone gets stiff or pushy. Feed separately, always, forever if needed. Resource guarders exist, and food is the usual court.

Supervise everything for the first weeks. Separate when you leave the house. Give the resident dog its own retreat, and honor it. The most common mistake is lavishing attention on the newcomer to reassure ourselves. Reassure the resident instead.

Cat into a dog household, or the reverse

This one runs on geography more than friendship. The cat needs vertical space the dog cannot reach, and every early meeting happens with the cat above the dog's nose or behind a barrier. Baby gates are the best invention this process has. Scent goes first, traded blankets, meals on opposite sides of a door. Face-to-face comes weeks later, dog on leash, calm, brief, and rewarded.

Some pairs become friends. Many become tolerant colleagues. A few will always need management. All three outcomes are successes if nobody is stressed.

The honest expectations

Watch for the good signs: parallel napping, play with role swaps, relaxed eating in shared space. Watch for the bad ones: the resident pet eating less, hiding more, or guarding spots it never guarded. Those mean slow down, not give up.

Time does the rest. The introduction is not an event you attend. It is a season you get through, and the reward for going slowly is a household that settles for good.

How do I introduce a new pet to my current pet?

Go slow: separate spaces first, swap scents, then short supervised meetings that end well. Species and personalities matter, so let relationships build over weeks.

What if my pets don't get along?

Do not force it. Separate and restart the scent-swap process more gradually, keep resources (food, beds, litter) separate, and consider a professional trainer if tension persists.
